# Product Leadership Role Guide

## 1. Select the archetype from evidence

Start with the approved job description, level framework, organization chart, product topology, and decision records. Decide whether the role is best described as a GPM player-coach, GPM people manager, product-area lead, Director managing PMs, Director managing managers, or portfolio leader. A hybrid is valid when each component has a documented scope and boundary.

Record title, level, reports-to role, product scope, people scope, geographic scope, time horizon, internal customers, expected outcomes, interfaces, exclusions, decision rights, and review cadence. Do not infer a management layer or seniority from title alone.

## 2. Separate product and people leadership

For product leadership, map discovery, strategy framing, roadmap integration, investment recommendations, planning, delivery oversight, launch governance, pricing input, lifecycle decisions, and outcome review. For people leadership, separately map hiring input, requisition approval, goal setting, coaching, performance review, compensation input, promotion, succession, workforce planning, and termination.

For every activity, identify who gathers evidence, proposes, recommends, decides, approves, executes, reviews, and receives escalation. Coaching PMs does not prove formal management authority. Coordinating a portfolio does not prove ownership of its strategy or investment budget.

## 3. Map staffing, budget, access, and external authority

Verify whether the role can request, allocate, or approve headcount; move people between teams; authorize contractors; manage a budget or P&L; approve procurement; access customer or employee data; modify roadmaps or systems; contact customers, partners, or analysts; or represent the organization externally. Record limits, thresholds, co-approvers, controls, and escalation paths.

Do not convert an employer example into a local entitlement. A published role framework may demonstrate that one organization assigns commercial or staffing duties, but it does not establish them elsewhere.

## 4. Define leadership measures without assigning outcomes

Potential review themes include strategy quality, portfolio coherence, evidence quality, decision latency, discovery health, team capability, leadership leverage, stakeholder alignment, delivery confidence, customer outcomes, and business outcomes. These are prompts, not assigned metrics or targets.

For each measure, record question, accountable owner, formula, numerator, denominator, unit, population, grain, period, filters, exclusions, source, refresh, target, quality limits, attribution limits, and interpretation. Separate role performance, team health, product outcomes, and business outcomes. Do not hold a leader solely accountable for revenue, adoption, retention, delivery, or employee outcomes without a documented accountability model and causal limits.

When metrics conflict, reconcile definitions, scope, timing, identity, aggregation, currency, attribution, and processing before choosing or explaining a value.

## 5. Produce evidence-first deliverables

A charter, scope map, decision-rights review, or metric brief should include a visible source note naming public archetype sources, local evidence used, dates or versions where available, and missing evidence. Label observations, assumptions, hypotheses, recommendations, and decisions separately. Record the accountable reviewer as `Needs verification` until provided.

Stop before personnel decisions, workforce commitments, investment approvals, roadmap publication, releases, pricing, system or access changes, customer or analyst contact, procurement, or external communication. Continue with the [workflow](workflow.md) and [quality check](evaluations/quality-check.md).
